# Annotation Guidelines — News Summarisation Evaluation
**Project**: LLM as a Judge · Evaluation Methods in NLP 2026  
**Task**: Rate two machine-generated summaries of a news article  
**Time estimate**: ~90 minutes for all 50 examples  

---

## What you are doing

You will read a news article excerpt and two summaries (labelled **A** and **B**) generated by two different automatic systems. You do not know which system produced which summary, and you should not try to guess — that is irrelevant to your job.

For each example you will assign a score on three dimensions and indicate an overall preference.

**Your judgements should be based solely on the article excerpt provided.** Do not use outside knowledge to fill gaps or verify facts.

---

## The rubric

Rate each summary on each dimension using the scale **1–5** (integers only, no decimals).

---

### Dimension 1 — Faithfulness
*Does the summary contain only information that can be verified in the article?*

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 5 | Every claim in the summary is directly supported by the article |
| 4 | Mostly supported; at most one minor imprecision (e.g. slightly wrong number) that does not change the meaning |
| 3 | One factual error or unsupported claim present; the overall gist is still correct |
| 2 | Multiple errors, or one significant hallucination (invented name, date, or event) |
| 1 | Summary contradicts the article or is largely invented |

**Guiding question**: *If I only read this summary, would I believe something that the article does not say?*

---

### Dimension 2 — Coverage
*Does the summary capture the most important information from the article?*

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 5 | All key facts and events are present and correctly emphasised |
| 4 | Most key information present; minor omissions only |
| 3 | Main point is present but important supporting detail is missing |
| 2 | Significant omissions; a reader would miss important facts |
| 1 | Summary misses the main point entirely |

**Guiding question**: *After reading only this summary, does a reader know what the article was about?*

---

### Dimension 3 — Fluency
*Is the summary grammatical and easy to read?*

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 5 | Perfect grammar; clear and natural to read |
| 4 | Minor stylistic issues but reads naturally overall |
| 3 | Some grammatical errors or awkward phrasing; still understandable |
| 2 | Frequent errors that noticeably impede reading |
| 1 | Largely incomprehensible or fragmented |

**Guiding question**: *Could this appear in a professional news outlet?*

---

### Overall preference

After scoring both summaries, indicate your overall preference:

- **A** — Summary A is better overall  
- **B** — Summary B is better overall  
- **tie** — Both are equally good (or equally bad)

This is a holistic judgement, not a mechanical average of the scores. It is fine for preference to be **tie** even if scores differ by 1 point.

---

## How to fill in the sheet

1. Open `annotation_sheet.csv` in Excel, Numbers, or Google Sheets.
2. **Add your name** in the `annotator_name` column for every row (or fill the column at the end using fill-down).
3. For each row, read:
   - `article_excerpt` — the first ~120 words of the article
   - `summary_A` — the full text of summary A
   - `summary_B` — the full text of summary B
4. Enter your scores for the 6 score columns (integers 1–5 only).
5. Enter `A`, `B`, or `tie` in `overall_preference`.
6. Use `notes` for anything you want to flag: ambiguous cases, very short summaries, articles that seem truncated mid-sentence, etc. This column is optional but valuable.

**Do not leave any score column blank.** If you are genuinely unsure between two values, pick the lower one and add a note.

---

## Important rules

- **Work independently.** Do not discuss individual examples with other annotators until all three of you have submitted.
- **Do not use the internet** to look up facts mentioned in the article. You are evaluating the summary against *the provided text*, not against ground truth.
- **Challenging examples** are tagged in the `is_challenging` column. These articles were deliberately selected because they are harder — involving numbers, many named entities, long texts, or nuanced contrasts. Expect more disagreement on these. Take a little extra time with them.
- **Do not skip examples.** If an article excerpt seems cut off (ends with `[…]`), score based on what is provided — the full article was used to generate the summaries.

---

## Edge cases

| Situation | What to do |
|-----------|-----------|
| Summary is a single sentence | Score normally; brevity alone does not lower coverage |
| Summary repeats a sentence twice | Penalise fluency by 1 point; note it |
| Summary mentions a name not in the excerpt but plausible | Score faithfulness 4, not 5 — you cannot verify it |
| Both summaries are identically bad | Score them independently; use `tie` for preference |
| Article excerpt ends abruptly | Score on what you have; note it |
